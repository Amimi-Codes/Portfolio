You find yourself in a tavern

Many want to play D&D. Few come out of the character creation process unscathed

Almost everyone I know has said "I've always wanted to try D&D" at some point. And then they open the Player's Handbook. Twelve base classes, a pile of species (and sub-species because who plays as a human right?), subclasses, backgrounds, ability scores, saving throws, proficiencies, spell slots… and that's all before you've rolled a single die.

Beginners don't struggle because the game is boring. They get stuck because every choice seems to need three other rules to understand it, and it's hard to tell which ones actually matter.

The rulebook way

  • Proficiency bonus
  • Hit dice
  • Cantrips
  • Saving throws
  • Subclass
  • Spell slots
  • Alignment
  • Ability modifiers
  • Background
  • Feats
  • Armor class
  • Sub-species
  • What is point buy?
  • Darkvision
  • Skill proficiencies

The BG3 way

  1. Pick who you are. Species and class shown, not described.
  2. See what it means. Only the details that matter at that step.
  3. Start playing. The rest unlocks as you go.

The inspiration

The BG3 approach

Many players are all too familiar with staring at a blank character creation sheet or the first step of creating a character on D&D Beyond, and feeling overwhelmed. Baldur's Gate 3 (BG3) has a different approach: it shows you the options visually, explains them only when you need them, and lets you watch your character take shape as you make each choice.

That's what I wanted for tabletop: the same depth, with a path through it that doesn't require reading 300 pages across 4 different books first.

BG3 is the model for how Origin feels and flows, not for its rules. The game adapts D&D and changes parts of 5e, so it isn't an official way to build a tabletop character. Origin follows the tabletop rules.

You leave town and find yourself in a forest

What adventurers told me

I've been gathering feedback from people who play D&D and people who have wanted to but never quite finished the character creation process, about where character creation loses them.

10 people answered the survey:

  • 4 new players, who have played once or twice
  • 4 experienced players
  • 2 Dungeon Masters
  • 2 of 4 new players stopped or put off creating their character
  • 3.3 vs 4.8 out of 5: how confident new and experienced players felt about playing their finished character in a game

Finding 01 · Two different bosses

New players fight the mechanics. Veterans fight the story.

Nobody new found choosing a class or species hard. They got stuck on the list-heavy steps that come after: three of four needed help with skills, and three of four named reading the rules as a problem. Experienced players breeze through the mechanics; what's left for them is the creative part, with four of six naming backstory, name or appearance as a sticking point.

Finding 02 · Party members wanted

New players learn from people and examples

All four new players found a friend helpful, and all four found a pre-made example character helpful. Across everyone, half named a person (a friend, the DM or their group) as their most useful source of help.

"Having someone actually there tailoring advice to your situation is the best way to learn hands-on."
"Creating with a group… helped having experienced people there who knew the lore."

Finding 03 · Lost in the woods

Beginners get lost in the rules

One new player never finished their character and another came back to it later. Their complaints were the maths, the time and not knowing what their choices meant. Even BG3, the friendliest creator in the survey, took one new player four hours. When I followed up, they said that time went into both building the character and customising its looks. The looks are outside Origin's scope; the building part is exactly what it should explain. What new players asked for was "actual instructions", and what a choice means once the game starts.

"There's so much math…"
"It's difficult for novice people to understand the intricacies of classes/species unless they've read thoroughly."
"Specific explanations on how choices could affect gameplay, such as what spells are useful in which scenarios."

Finding 04 · The last boss is the backstory

Even veterans want help with the story and the paperwork

Backstory was the most common recent difficulty overall (4 of 10), and three of those four were experienced players. Wishes included suggestions built from a character concept, help with lore, and help filling in a physical character sheet for in-person games.

"A platform to save all my details and AI to suggest things like background based on the character concept. Especially if I didn't have experienced players around."
"Something that helps fill out my physical character sheet. That always takes me the longest for in person one shots."

Loot · What this means for Origin

  • Spend the help where it hurts. Keep class and species quick; put guidance and recommended picks into skills, spells and equipment.
  • Be the friend at the table. Short, in-context answers to "what does this do?" instead of rule text.
  • Start from an example. Offer ready-made characters people can tweak instead of a blank sheet.
  • Show the payoff. Explain how each choice plays out in a game, and do the maths for them.
  • Help with the story too. Backstory prompts and suggestions that build on the character concept.
  • Finish on paper. Export to a printable character sheet, which is already on the quest map.
  • Keep the rules official. Stick to the current 5.5e rules and skip homebrew clutter.

Recounting past tales around the campfire

Every adventurer has a backstory. This one has three.

  1. Version 1 · ~2023

    The birth of Origin

    It started almost three years ago, before 5.5e was even born. Every species, class and stat was typed out by hand, and the design had the raw charm of a level 1 npc: it barely had any depth and had the same repeat dialogue for every species and class. And thus began the tale of Origin.

  2. Version 2 · Mimi's D&D Character Builder

    Character Development Arc

    Origin stayed stagnant and didn't make it out of the tavern... Then Baldur's Gate 3 came along. This project which had been collecting dust was starting to see movement. Our favourite "dating sim" game was now a beginners character creation tool, and it was inspiring me to dust Origin off and give it a second chance.

  3. Version 3 · In progress

    The Quest Begins

    A revamp is in the works, aiming to bring Origin up to speed with the latest D&D rules and BG3-inspired features. Levels were added, graphics were improved, and the user interface was streamlined.
